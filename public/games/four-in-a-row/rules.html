Four in a Row

Take turns dropping discs into an upright frame. The first to line up four of their own colour wins.

  • Players2 (or you against the computer)
  • Time5–10 minutes
  • Ages6 and up
  • Learn1 minute
  • Pass and playYes

Goal of the game

Be the first player to get four of your discs in a straight line: across, up and down, or on a diagonal. If the frame fills up and nobody has four in a line, the game is a draw.

What’s in the game

  • The frame: an upright blue rack with 7 columns and 6 rows, 42 holes in all. A disc dropped in at the top slides down its column.
  • Discs: one colour for each player, Red or Gold. There are enough to fill the whole frame, 21 each.

Setup

The frame starts empty. One player is Red and the other is Gold. At the start of every game the app picks at random who moves first, so sometimes Red starts and sometimes Gold does. The line above the board tells you whose move it is.

In many families one colour always goes first. Here the first move goes to a random player each game, so neither colour gets the head start every time.

How a turn works

  1. Pick a column. Any column that still has an empty hole at the top. You can’t pick a full column, and you can’t skip your turn.
  2. Drop your disc. It falls to the lowest empty hole in that column. You choose the column, never the row.
  3. Check for four. If your disc completes a line of four (or more) of your colour, you win straight away.
  4. Otherwise it’s the other player’s turn. Discs never move or come out again once they are in.

Winning and draws

A line of four can run in any of four directions. A line of five or more also counts. When it happens the four discs light up, a white line is drawn through them and the game ends.

If all 42 holes fill up and neither player has four in a line, the game is a draw. The board says “Draw — the board is full.”

Example game

Red moves first. Watch the bottom row.

  1. Red drops in the middle, column 4.
  2. Gold drops on top of it, also column 4.
  3. Red plays column 3. Red now has two side by side on the bottom row, with empty holes on both sides.
  4. Gold plays column 3, on top again. This is the mistake. Gold had to play next to Red’s pair, in column 2 or column 5.
  5. Red plays column 5. Now Red has three in a row on the bottom, and both ends (columns 2 and 6) are open: two threats at once.
  6. Gold blocks column 2. Gold can only block one end.
  7. Red drops in column 6: four in a row, columns 3 to 6. Red wins.

Table options

Four in a Row has no rule options: it is always the 7 × 6 frame, four in a line to win. When you start a game you can choose:

  • Computer players (Easy, Normal, Expert; default Normal):
    • Easy takes a win when it sees one, but plays a random column about a third of the time and doesn’t look at your threats.
    • Normal looks four moves ahead, counting both players’ moves.
    • Expert looks seven moves ahead and rarely misses a threat.
  • Game speed (Relaxed, Normal, Fast, Turbo; default Normal): how quickly the computer drops its disc.

All computer players prefer the middle columns when nothing else decides it.

Tips and strategy

Four in a Row takes a minute to learn, but there is a lot to it. These ideas go from first game to expert.

1. Every turn, ask three questions

  1. Can I win right now? Look for a hole that finishes four of yours and can be played this turn. If there is one, take it.
  2. Can my opponent win next move? If they have such a hole, block it now. Nothing else matters.
  3. What does my disc give away? Your disc becomes the floor for the next disc in that column. Don’t drop a disc right under a hole that would win for your opponent.

Most games between beginners are decided by a missed question 2, and most games between good players by question 3.

2. Take the middle

Not all holes are equal. A disc in the middle column can be part of many more lines of four than a disc at the edge, so it does more work for you and gets in your opponent’s way more.

Start in column 4 if you move first, and fight for columns 3, 4 and 5 in the opening. Edge columns are for blocking, not for building.
Four in a Row on this 7 × 6 frame has been solved by computer (in 1988): with perfect play the player who moves first can always win, and the winning first move is the middle column. Nobody plays perfectly, so both players have plenty of chances.

3. Threats

A threat is three of your discs in a line with the fourth hole of that line still empty. The empty hole is your threat hole. A threat hole is only an immediate danger when it is playable: on the bottom row, or with the hole under it already filled. A threat hole higher up is a long-term weapon: sooner or later someone has to fill the hole under it.

4. Forced blocks

When your opponent has a playable threat, your move is forced: you must drop into that hole. Forced moves work both ways. When you make a threat, you know exactly where your opponent’s next disc will go, so you can plan two moves at once. Good players make threats whose forced blocks put the opponent’s disc somewhere useless, or somewhere that lifts the column up to a hole they want.

Before you make a threat, look at the hole above the block. Your opponent’s blocking disc lets the next player use the hole on top of it — make sure that is not a winning hole for them.

5. Double threats win games

You can only block one hole per turn. So if a player has two playable threats at once (a double threat or fork), the other player can’t stop both. Making a fork is the most common way to win; stopping your opponent from making one is the most common way not to lose.

Two in a row on the bottom row with empty holes on both sides is already dangerous: one more disc makes an open three. Block it by dropping right next to the pair, not on top of it.

6. Stacked threats

Two threat holes of yours one directly above the other are just as deadly as a fork. When your opponent blocks the lower one, their own disc becomes the floor for your upper one.

7. Don’t fill the hole under a threat

If your opponent has a threat hole that isn’t playable yet, the hole under it is poison for you: drop there and they win on top of you. Under your own threat hole, a disc of yours lets your opponent block it. So both players leave those columns alone and play elsewhere, and the game becomes a battle over who runs out of safe moves first. That is the next section.

8. Watch the diagonals, and beating the Expert

Diagonal lines are the ones people miss. After every drop, look along both diagonals through the new disc. Against the Expert computer, short traps rarely work, because it looks seven moves ahead. Build threat holes high up in the frame on the right rows (see below), then let the frame fill up.

Advanced: odd and even rows

Number the rows 1 to 6 from the bottom. Every column has 6 holes, an even number, and the whole frame has 42.

  • The player who moved first drops discs number 1, 3, 5… The player who moved second drops discs 2, 4, 6…
  • Late in the game, when most columns are full and the rest of the frame fills up without a win, the first player ends up with the odd rows (1, 3, 5) of the last columns and the second player with the even rows (2, 4, 6). The simplest way to see it: if the second player always drops right on top of the first player’s disc, the second player gets every even-row hole.

So, as a rule of thumb:

  • If you moved first, aim for a threat hole on an odd row.
  • If you moved second, aim for a threat hole on an even row.

In the picture it is Gold’s move. Neither player wants to put a disc on row 2 of column 7. Both play elsewhere until the other columns are full. When only the top five holes of column 7 are left, 37 discs have been played. That is an odd number, so it is the second player’s turn: Gold has to drop into row 2, and Red wins on row 3. Being forced to make a move you don’t want to make is called zugzwang.

Not sure who moved first? When it is your turn and both colours have the same number of discs in the frame, you moved first.
This is the simple case: one threat, and nothing else happening on the board. With threats for both players in different columns it gets more complicated, but the rule of thumb (first player odd, second player even) is right far more often than not.

Quick reference

The game

7 columns, 6 rows. Two players, Red and Gold. A random player moves first.

Your turn

Drop one disc into any column that isn’t full. It falls to the lowest empty hole. No passing.

Win

Four or more of your colour in a line: across, up and down, or diagonal. The game ends at once.

Draw

All 42 holes full and no line of four.

Every move

1. Can I win? 2. Must I block? 3. Does my disc give them the hole above it?

Strategy

Take the middle. Make two threats at once. First player: threats on odd rows. Second player: even rows.

Glossary

Column
One of the 7 upright slots you drop discs into, numbered 1 to 7 from the left.
Row
One of the 6 levels of the frame. In this book, rows are counted 1 to 6 from the bottom.
Playable hole
An empty hole a disc would land in right now: on the bottom row, or with the hole under it filled.
Threat
Three of your discs in a line whose fourth hole is still empty. That empty hole is the threat hole.
Block
Dropping your disc into your opponent’s threat hole so they can’t finish the line.
Forced move
A move you must make, usually a block, because anything else loses at once.
Double threat (fork)
Two playable threat holes at the same time. Only one can be blocked.
Stacked threats
Two threat holes of the same player one directly above the other.
Odd and even threats
A threat hole on row 1, 3 or 5 (odd) or on row 2, 4 or 6 (even). Odd threats suit the player who moved first, even threats the player who moved second.
Zugzwang
Having to move when every move makes things worse, such as being forced to fill the hole under your opponent’s threat.
Draw
The frame is full and nobody has four in a line.
